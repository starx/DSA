# Motivation

When I was learning computing in school and college, I loved DSA. I loved learning all the different ways data could be stored, represented, and traversed. I loved algorithms because some of the solutions were genuinely mind-blowing. It was an intellectually exciting subject to learn.

But nearly 17 years down the line, I’ve forgotten most of it. I simply don’t use it. In fact, across all these years, I’ve had to rely on it only twice—once while writing a small personal app to help plan a journey, and once while supervising a sales pipeline at a company I worked for.

Why?

Because software engineering is primarily about dealing with requirements, people, processes, trade-offs between performance and quality, time and money constraints, debugging, refactoring, maintenance, and evolution of systems over time. Writing code is only one part of the job—and textbook DSA is a tiny fraction of that. Well under 1%.

Beyond that, I’ve never needed to implement these things myself. Modern programming languages and their ecosystems already provide highly optimized, well-tested implementations. Re-writing them would be worse, not better.

I do have strong fundamentals. I reason about complexity, I break problems into smaller parts, I reduce problem size with each iteration, and I avoid pathological patterns that cause systems to fall over at scale. Because of that, I rarely run into issues that require “clever” algorithms.

And yet—I hate this part of our industry.

Not learning DSA. I enjoyed that.

What I hate is that we use it to gauge the strength of a software engineer. I refuse to believe this measures anything particularly important about real-world software engineering.

People who build programming languages, low-level systems engineers, quant developers, and research engineers—absolutely. Their work genuinely depends on this depth.

But for the vast majority of software engineers, it doesn’t.

Still, I can’t skip it.

So here I am again—back on the donkey. And I’ll ride it.
